📖 How does this 2-phase DCF work?
Phase 1 (years 1–5): EPS grows at your phase-1 rate via compounding. Each year's EPS is discounted back to today using your required return.

Phase 2 (years 6–10): Growth slows to your phase-2 rate — more conservative, reflecting maturity. Still discounted back.

Terminal value: At year 10, the stock is valued at EPS × P/E. That price is discounted back to today.

Intrinsic value = sum of all discounted EPS (years 1–10) + discounted terminal value.

Why two phases? Most companies grow fast early, then slow down. Using one rate overestimates long-run earnings for growth stocks.
🎚 What inputs matter most?
  • Phase 1 growth rate: has the biggest impact. Be honest — analyst consensus is a good starting point.
  • Phase 2 growth rate: keep it below phase 1. For mature companies, 3–6% is realistic.
  • Target P/E: what multiple will the market pay in year 10? Use the current P/E as a reference — don't assume a big re-rating.
  • Required return: your personal hurdle rate. 10% is standard for equities. Use 12–15% for riskier stocks.
⚠ Limitations of this model
A DCF is only as good as its assumptions. This model:
  • Uses EPS rather than free cash flow — the gap matters for CapEx-heavy businesses
  • Assumes constant growth within each phase — no cyclical adjustments
  • Does not account for net debt, buybacks, or dilution
  • Terminal value still dominates (typically 50–70% of IV) — small P/E changes have large effects
Use as a reference framework, not as investment advice.
